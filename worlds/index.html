Think Complexity · all 12 chapters

Learn complexity by playing with it

  1. Each chapter is a small world made of blocks: a city, a party, a sand pile, a sugar-mountain society.
  2. You play first. Move households, introduce guests, drill rock, drop sand, before the computer takes over.
  3. Predict, then watch. Lock in a guess and the world shows you if you were right.
  4. Ask “why?” anywhere. Turn on ? Explain a block (bottom right) and tap any block: the world pauses and shows the rule behind it.
  5. Collect ideas. Every concept lands in your notebook, and the map shows how they connect.

Drag to spin the world, scroll or pinch to zoom, tap blocks to interact. Arrow keys move through a story.

Chapter 1 · Complexity science

Simple rules, surprising patterns

Schelling's city: red and blue agents on a grid, about 10% of houses empty. An agent is happy if at least N of its 8 neighbours match it; if not, it moves to a random empty house. Nobody asks for segregation, yet clusters appear and grow. Each tower's height shows how many like neighbours that agent has; floating, pale blocks are unhappy agents about to move.

Chapter 3 · Small world graphs

      
A few shortcuts shrink the world

      
Start with a ring lattice: each node linked to its k nearest neighbours. Clustering is high, but paths are long. Now rewire each edge with probability p. Shortcuts arch over the ring in yellow. At small p, path length collapses while clustering barely moves. That is Watts and Strogatz's small world, and the reason Milgram's letters needed only about six hops.

Chapter 4 · Scale-free networks

      
Growth plus rich-get-richer makes hubs

      
In the Facebook data, mean degree is 43.7 but the standard deviation is 52.4: most people have a few friends, one has over 1000. The WS model gets clustering and path length right but gives everyone about 44 friends. Barabási and Albert add two ingredients: nodes arrive one at a time, and each newcomer links to k existing nodes with probability proportional to their degree. Chapter 5 · Cellular automatons

      
A world in one line

      
A 1-D cellular automaton: a row of cells, each on or off. Every tick, each cell looks at itself and its two neighbours and uses an 8-entry rule table to decide its next state. Rows stack toward you, so the picture shows the world's whole history.

Famous rules

      

      
Rule 0 is class 1 (uniform). Rules 50, 18 and 90 are class 2 (nested). Rule 30 is class 3 (random-looking). Rule 110 is class 4 (complex, and Turing complete).

    

    

      
The shape of the argument

      

      
A simple model doesn't need to resemble the system in detail. It only needs the few features that are enough to produce the behaviour.

Chapter 9 · Agent-based models

      
Sugarscape: a society made of rules

      
Epstein and Axtell's artificial society. Each agent has a vision (1–6 cells), a metabolism (1–4 sugar per step) and some starting sugar. Each step it looks north, south, east and west, moves to the unoccupied cell with the most sugar, eats it, and burns its metabolism. Run out and it starves. Sugar grows back one unit per step. Out of this come inequality and migration waves.

Chapter 12 · Evolution of cooperation

      
Can selfish genes build nice brains?

      
The Prisoner's Dilemma: each player cooperates or defects. Both cooperate, 3 points each. Both defect, 1 each. One defects, they get 5 and the sucker gets 0. Defecting always pays more in a single round, yet mutual cooperation beats mutual defection. Strategies here remember the opponent's last two moves: 7 genes.
