## What does "95% confident" actually mean?

A food-delivery startup times 10 deliveries. The average is 33.1 minutes. Is the *real* average (over all deliveries, forever) 33.1? Almost certainly not exactly: a different 10 deliveries would give a different average. You learned in chapter 7 that sample averages wobble.

So instead of one number, we report a range: "between 27.9 and 38.3 minutes". The range is a **net** we throw at an invisible target. Here is the key idea, and it surprises most people:

> The 95% is a property of the **net-throwing method**, not of any one net. If you threw 100 nets this way, about 95 would catch the truth.

Any single net either caught it or didn't. You just don't know which. Throw 100 nets and see.

```quiz

* The method that produced 28 to 35 catches the true average in about 95% of samples
- If you repeat the study, 95% of new averages will land between 28 and 35
> The true average is a fixed number; it's either in [28, 35] or not. The 95% describes how often the *procedure* works. (The second option mixes up the average with single deliveries, which vary much more.)
```

## How much does an average wobble?

Before building any interval we need one number: how far a sample average typically lands from the truth. Commit to a guess first.

```predict
title: Deliveries have sd 8 min. Take 2000 samples of 25 deliveries each. What is the sd of the 2000 averages?
rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
samples = rng.normal(32, 8, size=(2000, 25))   # 2000 rows, 25 deliveries each
means = samples.mean(axis=1)
print("sd of single deliveries:", round(samples.std(), 1))
print("sd of the averages:     ", round(means.std(), 1))
```

The averages wobble 5 times less than single deliveries, and 5 = √25. That number has a name: the **standard error** (SE), the standard deviation of a statistic across samples:

**SE of the mean = σ / √n**

We never know σ, so we plug in the sample's own standard deviation `s` (with `ddof=1`, dividing by n − 1, because a sample's spread around *its own* average is a little too small).

Why √n and not n? Because errors partly cancel. Ten deliveries don't make the average 10 times more precise, only √10 ≈ 3.2 times. To halve the error you need **four** times the data. Check it:

```run
title: Quadruple n, halve the wobble
rng = np.random.default_rng(2)
for n in [25, 100, 400]:
    means = rng.normal(32, 8, size=(2000, n)).mean(axis=1)
    print(f"n = {n:4d}   sd of averages = {means.std():.2f}   formula 8/sqrt(n) = {8/np.sqrt(n):.2f}")
```

## Building the net: average ± 2 standard errors

From chapter 6: a normal value lands within 1.96 standard deviations of its centre 95% of the time. The sample average is (nearly) normal around the truth, with sd = SE. So the truth is within 1.96 SE of the average 95% of the time. Flip that around and you get the net:

**average ± 1.96 × SE**

If the "95% of the time" claim is real, we can *check* it by simulation: make many samples, build a net from each, count catches. Guess first.

```predict
title: We know σ = 8 here. Out of 10000 nets built as mean ± 1.96·8/√n, about what fraction catch 32?
rng = np.random.default_rng(3)
n, caught = 20, 0
for _ in range(10000):
    x = rng.normal(32, 8, n)
    m, se = x.mean(), 8 / np.sqrt(n)
    if m - 1.96 * se <= 32 <= m + 1.96 * se:
        caught += 1
print(round(caught / 10000, 2))
```

That is the honest meaning of "95%": it's a coverage rate you can measure. Other levels just change the multiplier: 1.645 for 90%, 2.576 for 99%. A higher level means a wider net, which is the price of missing less.

```parsons
?? se = sample.std(ddof=1) / len(sample)
---tests

```

```quiz
You switch from a 95% net (multiplier 1.96) to a 99% net (multiplier 2.576) on the same data. What happens?
- The interval gets narrower, because you're more confident
* The interval gets about 31% wider, and misses about 1 in 100 instead of 1 in 20
- Nothing changes; confidence only affects the wording
- The average moves closer to the truth
> More confidence costs width: 2.576 / 1.96 ≈ 1.31. The only way to get *both* narrow and confident is more data.
```

## Small samples need t

The z-interval quietly assumes we know σ. We don't; we use `s`, and with a small sample `s` itself wobbles. Sometimes it comes out too small, and the net shrinks just when it should be wide. How bad is that?

```predict
title: Same check, but n = 5 and we plug in s instead of the true σ. Fraction caught with 1.96?
rng = np.random.default_rng(4)
n, caught = 5, 0
for _ in range(10000):
    x = rng.normal(32, 8, n)
    m, se = x.mean(), x.std(ddof=1) / np.sqrt(n)
    if m - 1.96 * se <= 32 <= m + 1.96 * se:
        caught += 1
print(round(caught / 10000, 2))
```

A "95%" net that catches only about 88% of the time is lying. In 1908 William Gosset, a brewer at Guinness testing small batches of barley, worked out the fix: use a slightly bigger multiplier that depends on the sample size. It comes from the **t distribution** with n − 1 "degrees of freedom". For n = 5 the 95% multiplier is 2.78 instead of 1.96; by n = 30 it's 2.05, nearly the normal value.

```run
title: The t multiplier shrinks toward 1.96 as n grows
from scipy import stats
for n in [3, 5, 10, 30, 100]:
    print(f"n = {n:3d}   t multiplier = {stats.t.ppf(0.975, n - 1):.3f}")
print("normal       =", round(stats.norm.ppf(0.975), 3))
```

Why 0.975 and not 0.95? A 95% interval leaves 5% outside, split into **2.5% on each side**. So the upper cut is at the 97.5% point.

## The bootstrap: when there is no formula

σ/√n is the SE of a **mean**. What about the **median** delivery time, or the 90th percentile (the "slow" deliveries customers remember)? There's no tidy formula. The bootstrap, invented by Bradley Efron in 1979, says: your sample is your best picture of the population, so **resample from it** (with replacement, same size), recompute the statistic, and repeat a few thousand times. The spread of those recomputed values is the SE.

First, what does one resample look like?

```predict
title: Resample [1, 2, 3, 4, 5] with replacement. How many distinct values will it contain?
rng = np.random.default_rng(5)
data = np.array([1, 2, 3, 4, 5])
res = rng.choice(data, size=len(data), replace=True)
print(sorted(res.tolist()))
print("distinct:", len(set(res)))
```

Some values repeat and some vanish; on average a resample contains only about 63% of the distinct originals. That missing-and-doubling is exactly the variation a fresh sample would have. Why *with* replacement? Without it, every resample would be the original data shuffled, with the same median every time and zero spread: useless.

```run
title: Bootstrap the median of 15 deliveries
rng = np.random.default_rng(6)
times = np.array([22, 25, 26, 28, 29, 30, 31, 31, 33, 35, 38, 41, 44, 52, 67])
meds = np.array([np.median(rng.choice(times, len(times))) for _ in range(5000)])
lo, hi = np.percentile(meds, [2.5, 97.5])
print("sample median:", np.median(times))
print("bootstrap SE: ", round(meds.std(), 2))
print("95% bootstrap interval:", lo, "to", hi)
```

Taking the middle 95% of the bootstrap values is called the **percentile** interval. Back in the simulation, choose **bootstrap** and press **Show resamples of #1** to see the skyline of resample averages.

```exercise
def bootstrap_ci(data, stat, B=2000, conf=0.95, seed=0):
    data = np.asarray(data)
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    vals = np.array([stat(rng.choice(data, size=len(data), replace=True)) for _ in range(B)])
    tail = (1 - conf) / 2 * 100
    lo, hi = np.percentile(vals, [tail, 100 - tail])
    return (lo, hi)
---tests

lo3, hi3 = bootstrap_ci(np.full(10, 7.0), np.mean)
assert lo3 == hi3 == 7.0, "data with no spread gives an interval of zero width"
---hint

---hint
For 95%, the cut points are the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles: `np.percentile(vals, [2.5, 97.5])`.
```

## Planning: how many deliveries do we need?

The net's half-width is called the **margin of error**: about z × σ/√n. Turn that around and you can plan a study *before* collecting data: to get a margin of E you need n = (z·σ/E)².

```predict
title: With σ ≈ 8 min, how many deliveries for a 95% margin of ±1 min? And for ±0.5 min?
import math
for E in [2, 1, 0.5]:
    n = math.ceil((1.96 * 8 / E) ** 2)
    print(f"margin ±{E} min needs n = {n}")
```

Halving the margin quadruples the cost. That's the √n law again, and it's why polls stop at about 1000 people: going from ±3% to ±1.5% would need 4000.


## Real-world project: delivery times with honest error bars

```project
title: Delivery-time dashboard with error bars
client: A food-delivery startup promising "30 minutes or less"
skills: standard error, t-intervals, bootstrap, reporting uncertainty
---brief
The operations team has delivery times from each city zone, but some zones have 6 deliveries and some have 200. Last week they fired a courier team because "Zone C averaged 34 minutes", based on 7 orders. Build a report that gives each zone its average **and** a 95% interval, and only flags a zone as breaking the 30-minute promise when its *whole interval* is above 30.
---why
Every serious dashboard (A/B test tools, Google Analytics' confidence ranges, election forecasts, server latency monitors) shows error bars, because decisions made on noisy averages from tiny samples are mostly decisions made on luck. The bootstrap is how tools like these get intervals for percentiles (e.g. p95 latency) that have no formula.
---milestones
- Load each zone's delivery times into a dict of arrays
- Compute mean, SE and a 95% t-interval per zone
- Flag zones whose lower bound is above 30 as "late for sure", and those whose interval straddles 30 as "need more data"
- Add a bootstrap interval for each zone's 90th percentile (the slow deliveries)
- For zones that "need more data", estimate how many more deliveries would settle it
---stretch
- Plot the zones as a dot-and-whisker chart with matplotlib
- Check your t-intervals' coverage by simulating zones where you know the truth
- Compare t vs bootstrap intervals for a skewed zone with a few 60-minute disasters
```

```exercise
---prompt

---setup
```

```exercise
---starter
def t_ci(sample, conf=0.95):
    pass

    pass
---solution
def t_ci(sample, conf=0.95):
    x = np.asarray(sample, dtype=float)
    se = x.std(ddof=1) / np.sqrt(len(x))
    t = stats.t.ppf((1 + conf) / 2, len(x) - 1)
    return (x.mean() - t * se, x.mean() + t * se)

def boot_ci(sample, stat, B=2000, seed=0, conf=0.95):
    x = np.asarray(sample)
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    vals = [stat(rng.choice(x, len(x))) for _ in range(B)]
    a = (1 - conf) / 2 * 100
    lo, hi = np.percentile(vals, [a, 100 - a])
    return (lo, hi)
---tests
lo, hi = t_ci([30, 34, 28, 36, 31])
assert abs(lo - 27.83) < 0.01 and abs(hi - 35.77) < 0.01, f"expected about (27.83, 35.77), got ({lo:.2f}, {hi:.2f})"
rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
x = rng.normal(50, 10, 400)
blo, bhi = boot_ci(x, np.mean)
tlo, thi = t_ci(x)
assert abs(blo - tlo) < 0.5 and abs(bhi - thi) < 0.5, "for a big normal sample, bootstrap and t intervals for the mean should nearly agree"
---hint
t-interval: mean ± t.ppf((1+conf)/2, n−1) × s/√n. Bootstrap: resample with replacement, apply stat, take percentiles.
```

```recall
Q: Explain to a friend what "a 95% confidence interval of 28 to 35 minutes" means, why the interval narrows as n grows, and why small samples use t instead of 1.96.
A: The procedure that made [28, 35] catches the true average in about 95 of 100 samples; this particular interval either caught it or didn't. Its width is about 2 × multiplier × s/√n, so it shrinks like 1/√n (4x the data halves it). With small n the sample sd s is itself unreliable and often too small, so 1.96 would under-cover (about 88% at n = 5); the t multiplier is larger for small n to make the promise true. When no formula exists (median, percentiles), resample the data with replacement (the bootstrap) and take the middle 95% of the recomputed statistic.
```
