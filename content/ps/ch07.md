## Asking a few to learn about everyone

You want the average weekly exercise of a city of 900 people. Asking all of them is expensive, so you ask some. Whether that works depends far less on **how many** you ask than on **how you chose them**. Run three kinds of survey and compare.

```viz

height: 440

```

```quiz
A magazine surveys 2.4 million readers (huge!) and predicts the election wrong. A pollster asks 1,500 randomly chosen voters and gets it right. How?
- 1,500 is the magic number for polls
* The magazine's readers weren't like the voters as a whole; a biased sample stays biased however big it is
- Big samples have more random error
- The pollster got lucky
> This really happened: the *Literary Digest* in 1936 polled millions of its (richer-than-average) readers and called the election for the loser. Gallup's much smaller random sample got it right. Size cures random error, not bias.
```

## Population, sample, parameter, statistic

Four words to keep straight:

| | The whole group | What you compute |
|---|---|---|
| **Population** (all 900 residents) | | **Parameter**: the true average, unknown |
| **Sample** (the 50 you asked) | | **Statistic**: the sample average, your estimate |

A statistic is a random variable: ask a different 50 and you get a different number. So it has a distribution of its own, the **sampling distribution**, and that's what tells you how much to trust it.

```predict
rng = np.random.default_rng(3)
population = rng.exponential(4.0, 900)
print(round(population.mean(), 2))
for _ in range(3):
    sample = rng.choice(population, 50, replace=False)
    print(round(sample.mean(), 2))
```

## Good estimators: unbiased and tight

Judge an estimator by its sampling distribution:

- **Bias:** is it right *on average*? (Is the centre of its distribution the true value?)
- **Spread:** how much does it wobble from sample to sample? (Its standard deviation is the **standard error**.)

A famous example: estimating the population variance. Dividing the squared deviations by **n** comes out too small on average, because the sample mean sits closer to the sample's own points than the true mean does. Dividing by **n − 1** fixes it exactly. That's what `ddof=1` does in numpy.

```exercise

kind: write
---prompt
Write `average_variance_estimate(n, ddof, reps=20000, seed=0)`: draw `reps` samples of size `n` from a standard normal (true variance 1) using `rng.normal(size=(reps, n))`, compute each sample's variance with `np.var(..., ddof=ddof, axis=1)`, and return the **average** of those estimates.
---starter
def average_variance_estimate(n, ddof, reps=20000, seed=0):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    return 1.0
---solution
def average_variance_estimate(n, ddof, reps=20000, seed=0):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    x = rng.normal(size=(reps, n))
    return np.var(x, ddof=ddof, axis=1).mean()
---tests
biased = average_variance_estimate(5, 0)
fair = average_variance_estimate(5, 1)
assert abs(biased - 0.8) < 0.02, f"dividing by n with n = 5 is too small on average: about (n-1)/n = 0.8; got {biased:.3f}"
assert abs(fair - 1.0) < 0.02, f"dividing by n - 1 is right on average; got {fair:.3f}"
---hint
Generate the whole `(reps, n)` block at once, take the variance of each row, and average.
```

## Standard error: the size of random error

For a sample mean, the standard error is σ/√n (chapters 5 and 6), estimated with the sample's own standard deviation: `s / sqrt(n)`. It measures **random** error only: the wobble from *which* people you happened to ask. It says nothing about whether your way of asking was fair.

```exercise
title: standard_error
kind: fill
---prompt
Fill in the standard error of the mean, using the sample standard deviation with `ddof=1`.
---starter
def standard_error(sample):
    sample = np.asarray(sample, float)
    return ____
---solution
def standard_error(sample):
    sample = np.asarray(sample, float)
    return sample.std(ddof=1) / np.sqrt(len(sample))
---tests
s = [2, 4, 4, 4, 5, 5, 7, 9]
assert abs(standard_error(s) - np.std(s, ddof=1) / np.sqrt(8)) < 1e-12, "sd (ddof=1) over the square root of n"
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
means = [rng.normal(0, 3, 36).mean() for _ in range(5000)]
assert abs(np.std(means) - 0.5) < 0.02, "check: the real spread of means of 36 draws with sd 3 is 3/6 = 0.5"
---hint
`sample.std(ddof=1) / np.sqrt(len(sample))`
```

## The usual suspects: how samples go wrong

- **Convenience sampling:** asking whoever is easy to reach (people at the gym, your followers, students in your class).
- **Self-selection:** people choose whether to answer, and their choice is related to the answer (online polls, product reviews: the furious and the delighted write; the content stay silent).
- **Survivorship:** you only see the ones that made it through some filter (studying successful start-ups to learn what works, while the failed ones that did the same things are invisible).
- **Non-response:** the people who don't pick up the phone differ from those who do.

```quiz
In World War II, analysts mapped bullet holes on bombers returning from missions and proposed adding armour where the holes were densest. Abraham Wald said to armour where there were **no** holes. Why?
- Holes were randomly placed, so it didn't matter
* Planes hit in the hole-free areas didn't come back; the sample only contained survivors
- Armour is heavier in the places with holes
- Wald misread the data
> The returning planes are a survivorship-biased sample. Hits where you never see holes were the deadly ones. A huge sample of returning planes could never reveal that by itself.
```

## Fixing a lopsided sample: strata and weights

If you know how the population splits into groups (**strata**: age bands, regions, neighbourhoods) you can sample each group separately, or re-weight a sample so each group counts by its true share. Pollsters do this every election: if young voters are 20% of the electorate but 10% of the sample, each young respondent counts double.

```exercise
title: weighted_estimate
kind: write
---prompt
Write `weighted_estimate(sample, shares)`: `sample` is a list of `(group, value)` pairs, `shares` maps each group to its true share of the population (shares add to 1). Average the values **within** each group, then combine those group averages weighted by `shares`. Every group in `shares` must have at least one respondent; raise `ValueError` if one is missing (weighting can't invent people you never heard from).
---starter
def weighted_estimate(sample, shares):
    return sum(v for _, v in sample) / len(sample)
---solution
def weighted_estimate(sample, shares):
    groups = {}
    for g, v in sample:
        groups.setdefault(g, []).append(v)
    total = 0.0
    for g, share in shares.items():
        if g not in groups:
            raise ValueError(f"no respondents in group {g!r}")
        total += share * sum(groups[g]) / len(groups[g])
    return total
---tests
sample = [("young", 10)] * 10 + [("old", 2)] * 90
assert weighted_estimate(sample, {"young": 0.5, "old": 0.5}) == 6.0, "groups are half and half in reality: (10 + 2) / 2 = 6, even though the sample is mostly old"
assert abs(weighted_estimate(sample, {"young": 0.1, "old": 0.9}) - 2.8) < 1e-12, "if the sample's mix already matches reality, weighting changes nothing"
try:
    weighted_estimate(sample, {"young": 0.5, "middle": 0.2, "old": 0.3}); assert False, "should complain"
except ValueError:
    pass
---hint
Group the values with a dict of lists, then add up `share × group average` over the groups in `shares`.
```

```exercise
title: raw_vs_weighted
kind: write
---prompt
Write `raw_vs_weighted(trials=100, seed=0)`. In the simulated city, `young` are 40% of voters and support the candidate at 0.60; `old` are 60% and support at 0.40 (true support 0.48). Young people answer the phone with probability 0.1, old with 0.4. Each trial: call 2,000 random residents (pick each person's group with `rng.random() < 0.4` for young), keep those who answer, record their support (1 with the group's probability, else 0). Compute the raw average and the `weighted_estimate` (defined) with shares `{"young": 0.4, "old": 0.6}`. Return `(mean_raw, mean_weighted)` over the trials.
---setup
def weighted_estimate(sample, shares):
    groups = {}
    for g, v in sample:
        groups.setdefault(g, []).append(v)
    return sum(share * sum(groups[g]) / len(groups[g]) for g, share in shares.items())
---starter
def raw_vs_weighted(trials=100, seed=0):
    return 0.48, 0.48
---solution
def raw_vs_weighted(trials=100, seed=0):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    support = {"young": 0.60, "old": 0.40}
    answer = {"young": 0.1, "old": 0.4}
    raws, weighted = [], []
    for _ in range(trials):
        sample = []
        for _ in range(2000):
            g = "young" if rng.random() < 0.4 else "old"
            if rng.random() < answer[g]:
                sample.append((g, int(rng.random() < support[g])))
        raws.append(sum(v for _, v in sample) / len(sample))
        weighted.append(weighted_estimate(sample, {"young": 0.4, "old": 0.6}))
    return float(np.mean(raws)), float(np.mean(weighted))
---tests
raw, w = raw_vs_weighted()
assert raw < 0.45, f"the raw poll over-represents older residents, so it underestimates support; got {raw:.3f}"
assert abs(w - 0.48) < 0.01, f"weighting by the true age shares recovers the truth, 0.48; got {w:.3f}"
---hint
Inside each trial, build the list of `(group, vote)` pairs for the people who answered, then compute both estimates from it.
```

## Rebuild it and explain it

```exercise
title: sample_mean_and_se
kind: rebuild
---prompt
From memory: write `sample_mean_and_se(population, n, seed=0)` that draws a random sample of `n` **without replacement** from `population` (a numpy array) and returns `(mean, standard_error)` with the standard error computed from the sample (ddof=1).
---starter
def sample_mean_and_se(population, n, seed=0):
    pass
---solution
def sample_mean_and_se(population, n, seed=0):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    s = rng.choice(population, n, replace=False)
    return s.mean(), s.std(ddof=1) / np.sqrt(n)
---tests
pop = np.arange(10000, dtype=float)
m, se = sample_mean_and_se(pop, 400)
assert abs(m - pop.mean()) < 4 * pop.std() / 20, "the estimate lands within a few standard errors of the truth"
assert abs(se - pop.std() / 20) < 0.15 * pop.std() / 20, "the standard error is about σ/√400"
---hint
`rng.choice(population, n, replace=False)`, then mean and sd/√n.
```

```recall
Q: Your boss says "we got 50,000 responses to our customer survey, so the results must be accurate." What do you say?
A: 50,000 responses make the random error tiny, but they do nothing about bias. Who answered? If the people who chose to respond differ from customers overall (the very happy and the very angry are more likely to bother), the result is precisely wrong. A small random sample, or weighting the responses by known customer groups, would be more trustworthy than a huge self-selected one.
```
