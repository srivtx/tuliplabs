## You tested positive. Are you sick?

A disease affects 1 in 100 people. A test catches 90% of sick people, and wrongly flags 9% of healthy people. You test positive. What's the chance you have the disease?

Most people (and, in studies, most doctors) say about 90%. The real answer is about **9%**. Not a typo.

Instead of a formula, picture 1,000 people. The simulation shows them as blocks. Sick people are red, everyone takes the test, and **positives rise into the air**. The question is just: of the people in the air, how many are red?

## Natural frequencies: just count people

The trick that makes Bayes easy is to stop thinking in percentages and count **people** instead. Four numbers describe the whole situation:

- **Prevalence** (also called the **prior** or **base rate**): what fraction is sick before any test. 1%.
- **Sensitivity**: of the sick, what fraction test positive. 90%.
- **Specificity**: of the healthy, what fraction test *negative*. 91%, so 9% of the healthy get a false positive.
- The **posterior**: what we actually want, P(sick | positive).

Now push 1,000 people through it.

```predict
people = 1000
sick = people * 0.01
healthy = people - sick
true_pos = sick * 0.90
false_pos = healthy * (1 - 0.91)
print(round(sick), round(healthy))
print(round(true_pos), round(false_pos))
print(round(true_pos / (true_pos + false_pos), 3))
```

The false positives win because 9% of 990 is much bigger than 90% of 10. The test is fine; the disease is just rare.

## Bayes' rule is the same counting

Divide every count by the number of people and you get the formula known as **Bayes' rule**:

P(sick | +) = P(+ | sick) × P(sick) / P(+)

where P(+), the chance of testing positive at all, is the sum of both raised groups: P(+ | sick) × P(sick) + P(+ | healthy) × P(healthy). Top: the true positives. Bottom: all positives. It's exactly `true_pos / (true_pos + false_pos)`, with fractions instead of people.

The naive answer swaps the two conditionals: it reports P(+ | sick) (the sensitivity) when you asked for P(sick | +). Swapping them has a name, the **prosecutor's fallacy**, because it has put innocent people in prison ("only 1 in a million innocent people would match this evidence" is not "1 in a million chance he's innocent").

```exercise
assert posterior(0.3, 0.8, 1.0) == 1.0, "With specificity 100% there are no false positives, so a positive is certain"
---hint
Top = prior × sensitivity. Bottom = top + (1 − prior) × (1 − specificity).
```

## Why rare things fool good tests

Keep the test fixed and change only the prevalence. Same test, wildly different meaning.

```predict
def post(prior, sens=0.95, spec=0.95):
    tp = prior * sens
    fp = (1 - prior) * (1 - spec)
    return tp / (tp + fp)

for prior in [0.5, 0.1, 0.01, 0.001]:
    print(prior, round(post(prior), 3))
```

A 95%/95% test is nearly useless for screening a 1-in-1,000 disease on its own: 98% of positives are false alarms. That's why screening programmes use a cheap first test and then a **second, different test** on the positives. It's also why airport scanners, fraud alerts and smoke alarms "cry wolf" so often: the thing they look for is rare.

## Updating twice

You test positive, so you take a second, **independent** test, and it's positive too. What now?

Bayes chains: after the first test, your posterior (9%) becomes the **prior** for the second test. Feed it back in. Each positive multiplies the odds by the same factor, so belief climbs fast. Press **Test the positives again** in the simulation to see the false positives mostly drop out.

```predict
def update(prior, sens=0.9, spec=0.91):
    tp = prior * sens
    fp = (1 - prior) * (1 - spec)
    return tp / (tp + fp)

p = 0.01
for test in range(1, 4):
    p = update(p)
    print(test, round(p, 3))
```

```exercise
        if positive:
            like_sick, like_healthy = sens, 1 - spec

        p_new = prior * like_sick / (prior * like_sick + (1 - prior) * like_healthy)
    return p_new
---solution
def after_tests(prior, results, sens, spec):
    p = prior
    for positive in results:
        if positive:
            like_sick, like_healthy = sens, 1 - spec
        else:
            like_sick, like_healthy = 1 - sens, spec
        p = p * like_sick / (p * like_sick + (1 - p) * like_healthy)
    return p
---tests
one = after_tests(0.01, [True], 0.9, 0.91)
two = after_tests(0.01, [True, True], 0.9, 0.91)
assert abs(one - 0.0917) < 1e-3, f"one positive: about 0.092, got {one:.4f}"
assert abs(two - 0.5025) < 1e-3, f"two positives: about 0.50 (the 9% becomes the new prior), got {two:.4f}"
assert after_tests(0.01, [True, False], 0.9, 0.91) < one, "a negative after a positive should lower the belief"
assert after_tests(0.2, [], 0.9, 0.9) == 0.2, "no tests, no change"
---hint
Every update must start from the **latest** belief, not the original prior. One variable should be updated in place.
```

## From tests to words: spam

A spam filter is a medical test where the "disease" is spam and the "symptoms" are words. Say 40% of email is spam, the word "free" appears in 30% of spam and 2% of normal mail. You see "free". Same machine:

```predict
p_spam = 0.4
p_free_given_spam = 0.30
p_free_given_ham = 0.02
top = p_spam * p_free_given_spam
bottom = top + (1 - p_spam) * p_free_given_ham
print(round(top, 3), round(bottom, 3))
print(round(top / bottom, 3))
```

With many words, the filter updates once per word, as if each word were another independent test. Words aren't really independent ("free" and "prize" travel together), which is why it's called **naive** Bayes. It works surprisingly well anyway.

```quiz
Why do spam filters add up **logarithms** of probabilities instead of multiplying the probabilities?
- Logarithms make the answer more accurate in principle
* Multiplying hundreds of small numbers like 0.001 rounds to 0.0 in floating point; adding logs keeps the information
- Python can't multiply more than 100 numbers
- Logarithms remove the "naive" assumption
> log(a × b) = log a + log b, so the comparison is the same, but 0.001²⁰⁰ is 10⁻⁶⁰⁰, far below the smallest float (about 10⁻³⁰⁸). In logs it's just −1381.6, perfectly fine.
```

## Real-world project: a naive Bayes spam filter

```project
title: A spam filter that learns from examples
client: A tiny newsletter whose inbox is full of junk
skills: Bayes' rule, word counting, logarithms, smoothing
---brief
You get a handful of emails already labelled spam or ham (not spam). Learn, for each word, how often it appears in spam and in ham. For a new email, start from the prior P(spam) and update once per word. Two practical fixes make it work: **smoothing** (pretend every word was seen once more than it was, so a never-seen word doesn't give probability 0 and veto everything) and **logs** (add log-probabilities so nothing underflows).
---why
Naive Bayes was the engine of the first good spam filters (Paul Graham's "A Plan for Spam", 2002) and is still used as a fast baseline for text classification: support-ticket routing, sentiment, language detection. The same "prior × evidence" logic runs medical decision tools, fraud scores and A/B testing dashboards.
---milestones
- Split each email into lowercase words
- Count, for spam and for ham, how many emails contain each word
- Turn the counts into smoothed probabilities P(word | spam) and P(word | ham)
- Score a new email: log prior + sum of log likelihoods, for spam and for ham
- Classify the test emails and report how many it got right
---stretch
- Print the 5 words that push hardest towards spam (biggest log ratio)
- Measure false positives (ham marked spam) separately: they're the costly mistake
- Try it on a real labelled dataset, such as the SMS Spam Collection
```

```exercise
---solution
def spam_probability(text, p_spam, p_word_spam, p_word_ham):
    spam_score = math.log(p_spam)
    ham_score = math.log(1 - p_spam)
    for w in set(text.lower().split()):
        if w in p_word_spam:
            spam_score += math.log(p_word_spam[w])
            ham_score += math.log(p_word_ham[w])
    return 1 / (1 + math.exp(ham_score - spam_score))
---tests

assert abs(spam_probability("zebra", *model) - 3 / 7) < 1e-9, "unknown words carry no evidence: the answer is just the prior 3/7"
long_spam = " ".join(f"prize{i}" for i in range(400)) + " free prize"
assert 0 <= spam_probability(long_spam, *model) <= 1, "must not crash or underflow on long emails"
```

## Rebuild it and explain it

```exercise
title: bayes
kind: rebuild
---prompt
From memory: write `bayes(prior, like_if_true, like_if_false)` returning P(hypothesis | evidence), where the likelihoods are P(evidence | hypothesis true) and P(evidence | hypothesis false).
---starter
def bayes(prior, like_if_true, like_if_false):
    pass
---solution
def bayes(prior, like_if_true, like_if_false):
    top = prior * like_if_true
    return top / (top + (1 - prior) * like_if_false)
---tests
assert abs(bayes(0.01, 0.9, 0.09) - 0.0917) < 1e-3, "the medical test: about 9%"

---hint
Top: the "true and evidence" share. Bottom: everyone with the evidence.
```

```recall
Q: A test is 99% sensitive and 99% specific, and the condition affects 1 in 10,000 people. Explain, with counts of people, why a positive result still probably means you're healthy.
A: Take 1,000,000 people: 100 are sick and 99 of them test positive. 999,900 are healthy and 1% of them, about 9,999, also test positive. So of roughly 10,098 positives only 99 are sick, under 1%. The base rate is so low that the small false positive rate applied to the huge healthy group swamps the true cases. The fix is a second independent test on the positives: the posterior becomes the new prior.
```
