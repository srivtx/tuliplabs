## What does "1 in 6" mean?

A die has a "1 in 6 chance" of showing a 4. But roll it six times and you almost never get exactly one 4. So what is that 1 in 6 a promise about?

It's a promise about the **long run**. Roll a thousand times and close to a sixth of the rolls are 4s. Roll a million times and it's closer still. Nobody can predict one roll, but a pile of rolls is very predictable.

Before any formulas, roll some dice. Start with **two dice** and look at the shape the sums make.

## Outcomes, sample spaces and events

Three words do all the work in probability:

- An **outcome** is one thing that can happen: the die shows 4.
- The **sample space** is the list of *every* outcome: {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6}.
- An **event** is a group of outcomes you care about: "an even number" is {2, 4, 6}.

When every outcome is **equally likely**, the probability of an event is just counting: how many outcomes are in the event, divided by how many there are in total. Even number: 3 out of 6, so 1/2.

For two dice, the sample space is every *pair*. `itertools.product` builds it for you.

```predict
space = list(itertools.product(range(1, 7), repeat=2))
print(len(space))
print(space[:3])
sevens = [pair for pair in space if sum(pair) == 7]
print(len(sevens))
print(len(sevens) / len(space))
```

Why pairs, and not just the 11 sums? Because the *pairs* are equally likely and the sums are not. The red ghost in the simulation is what you get if you forget this.

    return hits / len(space)

## Probability is a long-run frequency

Counting works when the outcomes are equally likely and few. When they aren't, we **simulate**: do the experiment many times on the computer and count.

`np.random.default_rng(seed)` makes a random number generator. Giving it a **seed** means you get the same "random" numbers every run, so you can check results. `rng.integers(1, 7, size=n)` rolls `n` dice (the 7 is *excluded*, like `range`).

```predict
rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
rolls = rng.integers(1, 7, size=10)
print(rolls)
for n in [10, 1000, 100000]:
    rolls = rng.integers(1, 7, size=n)
    print(n, round(float(np.mean(rolls == 4)), 2))
```

Notice `rolls == 4` makes an array of `True`/`False`, and the mean of that is the **fraction** of rolls that were 4. That one line is the workhorse of this whole track.

## Counting without listing

Listing every outcome stops working fast: a 5-card poker hand has millions. So we count with three rules.

1. **Multiplication rule.** If you make one choice with `a` options and then another with `b` options, there are `a × b` combinations. Two dice: 6 × 6 = 36. A 4-digit PIN: 10 × 10 × 10 × 10.
2. **Permutations** (order matters, no repeats). Gold, silver and bronze among 8 runners: 8 × 7 × 6 = 336. That's `math.perm(8, 3)`.
3. **Combinations** (order doesn't matter). Picking 3 people for a team from 8: each team was counted 3 × 2 × 1 = 6 times in the permutations (once per ordering), so 336 / 6 = 56. That's `math.comb(8, 3)`.

```predict
print(10 ** 4)
print(math.perm(8, 3))
print(math.comb(8, 3))
print(math.perm(8, 3) // math.factorial(3))
print(math.comb(52, 5))
```

The question to ask every time: **does the order matter?** Medals: yes (gold ≠ silver). A team or a poker hand: no.

## The birthday problem

How many people do you need in a room before there's a better than even chance that two share a birthday? Most people guess around 180. The real answer is **23**.

Counting trick: it's easier to find the chance that **nobody** shares. The first person can have any of 365 days, the second must avoid that one (364 left), the third must avoid two (363 left), and so on. Then the chance someone *does* share is 1 minus that. (Using "1 minus the opposite" is called the **complement**; it gets its own step next chapter.)

```predict
p_none = 1.0
for i in range(23):
    p_none *= (365 - i) / 365
print(round(p_none, 3))
print(round(1 - p_none, 3))
```

Why so few? Because with 23 people there are `math.comb(23, 2)` = 253 *pairs*, and any pair can match. You were probably picturing someone matching *you*, which is only 22 pairs.

Now check it the simulation way: give 23 people random birthdays, many times, and count the rooms with a repeat.

def shared_birthday_rate(people, trials, seed=1):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    hits = 0

        days = rng.integers(0, 365, size=people)

## Real-world project: a lottery odds checker

Lotteries print "odds of winning" on the ticket. Are they honest? You now have every tool to check.

```project
title: Lottery odds checker
client: A consumer-rights blog checking what lottery tickets claim
skills: combinations, complement, simulation with numpy, comparing formula vs simulation
---brief
In a "6 from 49" lottery you pick 6 different numbers from 1–49, and 6 winning numbers are drawn. Prizes depend on how many of yours match. Build a checker that gives the exact chance of matching exactly k numbers (k = 0…6), then **confirms it by simulating** a million draws. If formula and simulation disagree, one of them is wrong, and you'll want to know which before you publish.
---why
The same counting is behind every "1 in N" you see: password strength meters (how many guesses to brute force), card games and casino audits, A/B test sample planning, and quality checks ("pick 5 items from a batch of 100, what's the chance we miss all 3 bad ones?", which is exactly this formula).
---milestones
- Count all possible tickets with `math.comb(49, 6)` and compare it with the jackpot odds printed on a real ticket
- Count tickets with exactly k matches: choose k of the 6 winners and 6−k of the 43 losers
- Check that your probabilities for k = 0…6 add up to 1
- Simulate many draws with numpy and compare each k with the formula
- Write one sentence for the blog: "A ticket matches at least 3 numbers about 1 time in ___"
---stretch
- Add a bonus ball and compute the "5 + bonus" prize odds
- Price check: if a ticket costs 2 and the prizes are known, what does a ticket return on average? (Chapter 5 gives you the tool.)
- Plot formula vs simulation as two bar charts side by side
```

The key idea: a ticket with exactly `k` matches picks `k` of the 6 winning numbers **and** `6 − k` of the 43 losing numbers. Those two choices multiply.

```exercise
title: match_probability
kind: write
---prompt
Write `match_probability(k, n=49, pick=6)`: the chance that a ticket of `pick` distinct numbers from 1..`n` matches **exactly** `k` of the `pick` winning numbers. Use `math.comb`.
---starter
def match_probability(k, n=49, pick=6):
    pass
---solution
def match_probability(k, n=49, pick=6):
    ways = math.comb(pick, k) * math.comb(n - pick, pick - k)
    return ways / math.comb(n, pick)
---tests
assert abs(match_probability(6) - 1 / 13983816) < 1e-15, "The jackpot: 1 in 13,983,816"
assert abs(sum(match_probability(k) for k in range(7)) - 1) < 1e-12, "k = 0..6 covers every ticket, so the probabilities must add up to 1"
assert abs(match_probability(3) - 0.017650) < 1e-5, f"Exactly 3 matches is about 1.77%, got {match_probability(3):.6f}"
assert abs(match_probability(1, n=10, pick=2) - 16 / 45) < 1e-12, "Small check: 2 from 10, exactly 1 match = 2*8/45"
---hint
Ways to match exactly k = (choose k of the winners) × (choose the rest from the losers): `math.comb(pick, k) * math.comb(n - pick, pick - k)`.
```

```exercise
title: simulate_matches
kind: write
---prompt
Now the proof. Write `simulate_matches(trials, seed=0)` that plays `trials` draws: each time, draw 6 winning numbers and 6 ticket numbers (each **without repeats** from 1..49, use `rng.choice(np.arange(1, 50), size=6, replace=False)`), and count the matches. Return a list `freq` of length 7 where `freq[k]` is the fraction of draws with exactly `k` matches.
---setup
def match_probability(k, n=49, pick=6):
    return math.comb(pick, k) * math.comb(n - pick, pick - k) / math.comb(n, pick)
---starter
def simulate_matches(trials, seed=0):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    counts = [0] * 7
    # your code here
    return [c / trials for c in counts]
---solution
def simulate_matches(trials, seed=0):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    numbers = np.arange(1, 50)
    counts = [0] * 7
    for _ in range(trials):
        win = rng.choice(numbers, size=6, replace=False)
        ticket = rng.choice(numbers, size=6, replace=False)
        k = len(set(win) & set(ticket))
        counts[k] += 1
    return [c / trials for c in counts]
---tests
f = simulate_matches(20000)
assert len(f) == 7 and abs(sum(f) - 1) < 1e-9, "freq needs 7 entries that add up to 1"
for k in range(3):
    assert abs(f[k] - match_probability(k)) < 0.012, f"k={k}: simulation {f[k]:.4f} vs formula {match_probability(k):.4f} should agree"
assert abs(f[3] - match_probability(3)) < 0.004, "About 1.8% of draws should have exactly 3 matches"
---hint
`set(win) & set(ticket)` is the set of numbers on both. Its length is the number of matches.
```

## Rebuild it and explain it

```exercise
title: birthday
kind: rebuild
---prompt
From memory: write `p_shared(n)`, the exact probability that among `n` people at least two share a birthday (365 equally likely days, ignore leap years). Use the complement.
---starter
def p_shared(n):
    pass
---solution
def p_shared(n):
    p_none = 1.0
    for i in range(n):
        p_none *= (365 - i) / 365
    return 1 - p_none
---tests
assert abs(p_shared(23) - 0.5073) < 1e-3, f"23 people: about 0.507, got {p_shared(23)}"
assert p_shared(1) == 0, "One person can't share"
assert p_shared(366) == 1, "366 people must share (the factor (365-365)/365 is 0)"
assert p_shared(50) > 0.97, "50 people: about 97%"
---hint
Person i (counting from 0) must avoid the i birthdays already taken: multiply by `(365 - i) / 365`. Then subtract from 1.
```

```recall
Q: In your own words: why are the 11 sums of two dice not equally likely, and how would you check a probability like P(sum = 7) without any formula?
A: The equally likely things are the 36 (die 1, die 2) pairs, not the sums. Six pairs make 7 but only one makes 2, so 7 is six times as likely. To check without a formula, simulate: roll two arrays of dice many times with numpy, add them, and take the mean of `sums == 7`. With enough rolls the frequency settles on the probability (about 0.167).
```
